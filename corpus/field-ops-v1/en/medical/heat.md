# Field protocol 7.7 — Heat illness recognition and response

**Synthetic exercise document. Not medical advice.** This benchmark artifact imitates an emergency operations handbook and must not replace current public-health or clinical guidance.

## Operating context

Use this protocol during high-heat shelter operations, outdoor distribution, debris clearance, or prolonged work in protective clothing. Supervisors establish shaded rest areas, drinking-water access, a buddy system, and a work/rest cycle before symptoms appear. New arrivals, older adults, children, pregnant people, people taking certain medicines, and workers not acclimatized to heat may need more frequent checks. A normal-looking person can still deteriorate quickly.

## Initial assessment

Stop activity and move the affected person away from heat. Ask their name, location, and what happened; abnormal answers are a warning sign. Heat exhaustion commonly includes heavy sweating, weakness, headache, nausea, dizziness, irritability, and cool or clammy skin. Heat stroke is suspected when heat exposure is paired with central nervous system changes such as confusion, unusual behavior, loss of coordination, seizures, or unconsciousness. Skin may be hot and either wet or dry; absence of dry skin does not rule it out.

## Heat exhaustion pathway

Move the person to an air-conditioned or shaded cool place, loosen or remove unnecessary outer clothing, and apply cool wet cloths while fanning. Cool packs may be placed at the neck, armpits, and groin with a cloth barrier. If the person is fully alert, can swallow normally, and is not vomiting, offer small repeated sips of cool water or an appropriate oral rehydration drink. Do not force rapid intake. Stop oral fluids if alertness or swallowing worsens. Arrange medical review if symptoms are severe, persist, recur, or the person has significant underlying illness.

## Suspected heat-stroke pathway

Confusion, collapse, a seizure, loss of consciousness, or very hot skin makes the situation time-critical. Call emergency services immediately and begin rapid cooling while help is moving. Remove excess clothing, wet the skin with cool water, fan continuously, and rotate cold wet towels. Use cold-water immersion only when the site has a trained team able to protect the airway and monitor the person. Do not give fluids to anyone who is confused, having a seizure, vomiting repeatedly, or unconscious. Place an unresponsive but breathing person in a position that protects the airway; begin resuscitation if they are not breathing normally and follow dispatcher instructions.

## Coordination and documentation

The communications responder reports the exact site entrance, patient count, mental status, cooling started, and hazards. Record symptom onset, last known normal behavior, work duration, fluids taken, cooling methods, and changes every five minutes. One responder remains responsible for observation during transfer. The supervisor also checks coworkers from the same crew because a cluster may indicate a failed work/rest or hydration plan.

## Return-to-work boundary

No person with altered mental status returns to duty. A person whose mild symptoms resolve is not automatically cleared: record the event, arrange evaluation under local policy, and correct environmental controls before the crew resumes work. The operational review examines shade, water, work pacing, protective equipment, staffing, and whether the buddy check detected symptoms promptly.
