# Field note 2.8 — Emergency household water chlorination

**Synthetic exercise document. Not public-health guidance.** The numerical values are included to test precise retrieval from a longer operational note. Users must consult current authorities for real emergencies.

## Decision boundary

Use the best available known-safe source. Chlorination is a fallback considered only when boiling is not practical and when the suspected hazard is biological. It does not make water contaminated by fuel, pesticides, industrial chemicals, radioactive material, or harmful salts safe. Water with a chemical odor, oily sheen, unusual source, or an official “do not use” order is excluded and should be reported to the incident authority.

Choose regular unscented household bleach whose label states 5–9% sodium hypochlorite. Do not use scented, splashless, color-safe, gel, combined cleaner, or swimming-pool products. Do not use a product with an unreadable label or unknown concentration. Keep the product away from children and never mix it with ammonia, acids, or other cleaners.

## Preparation and dose

Use a clean container and the most accurate dropper available. If water is cloudy, colored, or contains particles, first allow sediment to settle and filter the upper water through a clean cloth, paper towel, or coffee filter. This improves clarity but is not the disinfection step.

For clear water, add 8 drops of qualifying bleach per U.S. gallon. For water that remains cloudy or colored, or is very cold, add 16 drops per gallon after filtering. For multiple gallons, multiply carefully and have a second person verify the calculation. Stir thoroughly, cover the container, label it with treatment and ready times, and wait at least 30 minutes before use. Do not shorten contact time because the water looks clear.

After the contact period, the water should have a slight chlorine odor. This simulated field note directs staff with no odor to repeat the same dose once and wait an additional 15 minutes; persistent uncertainty is escalated and the batch is not distributed. Excess chemical is not a substitute for measurement.

## Distribution controls

Treated and untreated containers remain physically separated. Each distributed batch receives a lot identifier tied to source, volume, product label, concentration, dose, preparer, verifier, treatment time, and release time. Use clean covered containers; avoid touching the inside of caps or dipping used cups into shared storage. A dispensing tap is preferred.

The distribution message states that this is an emergency biological-risk measure and identifies people who should follow current clinical or public-health advice. Staff do not describe chlorinated water as sterile. Complaints about taste, illness, product concentration, or possible chemical contamination are logged against the batch and escalated.

## Calculation examples and errors

A five-gallon container of clear water receives 40 drops under the stated assumptions. A two-gallon container of very cold, cloudy water receives 32 drops after filtration. Common errors include confusing drops with teaspoons, treating liters as gallons, using an unknown concentrated product, failing to start the contact-time clock after mixing, and topping up a labeled batch with untreated water.
